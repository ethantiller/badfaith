Senate clears funding bill after a week of brinkmanship

By a staff reporter

The Senate voted 62 to 37 on Tuesday evening to advance a funding package that keeps federal agencies open through the end of March, ending a standoff that had run for the better part of a week and had begun to worry agency administrators who were drawing up contingency plans.

Supporters of the measure described the vote as a return to ordinary business, arguing that the alternative was a shutdown nobody in either party wanted to own. Critics called the package a reckless scheme that postpones every difficult decision by ten weeks and does nothing at all to address the underlying spending disagreement.

Unemployment fell to 3.8 percent in August, according to figures the Labor Department released last week, and the administration has pointed to that number repeatedly in defending its budget position. Independent economists note the figure excludes several categories of seasonal worker, and say the comparison with last year is not as clean as it has been presented.

"Nobody wins a shutdown," the majority leader told reporters shortly after the vote, standing in a corridor outside the chamber. "We can argue about the numbers in March. Right now the country needs its government open, and that is the whole of it." Several members of his own caucus had spent the previous day arguing the opposite.

The package now moves to the House, where the leadership has signalled it will bring the measure to a vote before the deadline on Friday. Whether it survives there in its current form is far from settled, and at least a dozen members have said publicly that they will not support it without changes to the agriculture provisions.

Agency administrators, meanwhile, had already begun issuing the notices that precede a lapse in funding. Those notices were withdrawn within hours of the Senate vote, according to two officials who were not authorised to discuss the preparations and spoke on condition of anonymity.

Every American understands what is at stake here, one senator argued during the floor debate, and anyone who claims otherwise has not been paying attention to what ordinary families are going through. The remark drew applause from one side of the chamber and silence from the other.

The March deadline sets up the same argument again, with the same participants and a shorter runway. Both sides said afterwards that they expected to be back in the same corridor, making the same arguments, before the spring.
